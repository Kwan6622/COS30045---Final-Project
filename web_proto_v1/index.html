    People walking and cycling, hospitalised after road crashes · Australia 2011–2021

    Every day, … hospital beds hold someone who was walking or cycling.

    News about road safety counts deaths. Many more people survive a crash and end up in hospital. This story is about the people with no car around them: pedestrians and cyclists.

    What it means

    In 2021, … hospital stays began with someone on foot or on a bike: about one every … minutes.

    What is being done

    Australia's National Road Safety Strategy aims to cut serious injuries by at least 30% by 2030 . Hospital stays like these are what that target is about.

    What it means

    From 2012 to 2019, cyclist admissions rose …. Pedestrian admissions barely moved (…). Nothing here shows walking getting safer. We do not know how many people cycle, so part of the rise may simply be more cycling.

    What can be done

    More people riding needs more safe space to ride and walk. The WHO lists footpaths, cycling lanes, safe crossing points and traffic calming as key ways to reduce injuries for these road users .

    What it means

    A pedestrian stays … days on average; someone in a car … days. … of pedestrians admitted die within 30 days (car occupants …). … of injured pedestrians collided with a car, pick-up or van, while … of injured cyclists had no collision at all, such as a fall.

    What can be done

    Speed decides how badly a pedestrian is hurt. The risk of death is about 5% at an impact speed of 30 km/h and 50% at 59 km/h, so researchers support 30–40 km/h limits where many people walk . For cyclists, many injuries involve no other vehicle, which points to the road itself: cycling lanes and traffic calming .

    Chapter 3 · It is someone's grandparent

    Older pedestrians are hurt the worst

    Each bar is an age group. The 75+ bar is highlighted. Use the buttons to switch between pedestrians and cyclists, and between three measures.

    Switch to Cyclists: is it the same age group?

    What it means

    A pedestrian aged 75 or over stays about … days in hospital, and … dies within 30 days of admission. Older people are more frail and find it harder to judge the speed and distance of traffic .

    What can be done

    Transport Victoria advises older pedestrians to cross at traffic lights or pedestrian crossings, cross as few roads as possible, wear brightly coloured clothing, have sight and hearing checked regularly, and ask a pharmacist how medicines may affect them on the road . Share this with the older people in your life.

    What it means

    Pedestrian admissions peak in June (… above an average month); cyclists peak in March. Darker evenings may play a part, but this data cannot prove it. Per person, the Northern Territory is highest for pedestrians and the ACT for cyclists.

    What can be done

    Be seen when it is dark: wear brightly coloured clothing when walking , and a bike ridden at night must have a white front light and a red rear light . Governments should compare rates per person, not raw counts, when choosing where to act first.

    References and data notes

    About the data

    • An admission is not a person. BITRE counts hospital admissions; one person can be counted more than once.
    • No fault. The data records what the injured person collided with, not who caused the crash.
    • Counting changes. Victoria (from 2012) and NSW (from 2017) changed what counts as an admission, so we compare 2012 with 2019.
    • COVID. Travel in 2020 and 2021 was unusual, so those years are shaded or left out.
    • No exposure data. We do not know how many people walk or cycle, so a rise in cyclist injuries may partly reflect more cycling.
    • Who is missing. People killed at the scene are not in hospital data. Patients who died within 30 days are reported separately.
    • Scope. First Nations data was left out: a narrow treatment without community context risked misrepresenting First Nations people.
